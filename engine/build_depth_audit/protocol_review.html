TESTAHIL · 23 Aug 2026 · review of the governing documents

Are the rules enough to stop this happening again?

A read of Standing_Research_Protocol.md and the condensed digest against the failures they were written to prevent — and against what the repository actually does today.

the answer

Clear: yes. Preventive: no — not yet. As written rules these two documents are better than anything I would suggest replacing them with. Every rule carries the failure that produced it, which is rare and worth protecting. Nothing important is missing from the text.

What they lack is teeth. They are addressed to a careful reader, and every failure in this repository’s history happened to a careful reader — not to someone who disagreed with a rule, but to someone who never met it at the moment it bound. The composite beta spread through the entire book while the rule against it was already written down. That is the whole problem in one sentence, and it is still true of eight of the nine SIGCM clauses today.

What is actually wrong

Seven findings, each checked against the repository rather than inferred from the text.

Critical

The hole the protocol already diagnosed is still open in eight places

The protocol says this about itself, in the beta section: “SIGCMChecklist.beta_own_history_vs_egx30 is a flag a study sets itself, and every study set it True while regressing on a composite. A self-attestation cannot catch that.” That diagnosis is exactly right, and it was acted on — assert_beta_provenance() now inspects the actual beta record instead of trusting the flag.

But SIGCMChecklist has nine clauses and only that one was fixed. The other eight are still plain booleans the study sets on itself, including forecast_ground_up — the ground-up construction rule. The build-depth audit found 63 of 90 studies are not built ground-up. The same hole, one clause over, and nothing has been looking through it.

Critical

No rule anywhere requires a study to run the checks

Searched both governing documents for any requirement that a study call assert_sigcm(), assert_beta_provenance() or assert_model_study(). There is none. The QC gate says a violation is a hard fail, but nothing says the study must look. 13 of the 21 study directories accordingly call nothing, and until today no automated job ran any of them. A study passed by not checking itself.

High

The beta record has no required shape, so the gate can only inspect what happens to be there

Across the 21 study directories the regressor is recorded four different ways: a full raw_indices/ path, a bare filename, a prose name with no file at all, and nothing. assert_beta_provenance() is only as strong as the record it reads, so the weakest shape silently defeats it. Three studies this audit first read as clean fail the stricter test for exactly this reason.

High

“Never hand-roll a study-local beta script” is prose with nothing behind it

The rule is unambiguous and it postdates the composite correction. Two studies written after it still carry a beta_reg.py that runs its own np.linalg.lstsq regression rather than calling beta_regression.own_stock_beta(), and one of them still builds a composite alongside it as a “corroboration”. Nothing was watching.

High

There is no version on the standard, so no study can say what it was built to

Nothing in either document stamps a study with the standard it was written against. That is why the question “is this study finished or provisionally finished?” has no answer in the repository, and why a name re-issued in September could need re-issuing again in November. A standard_version in every study record, and a stated current version in the protocol, turns the rebuild queue from an open-ended obligation into a finite one.

Medium

The index registry is not canonical, so a duplicate could sit in it unnoticed

ADXGENERAL.csv and FADGI.csv are the same series under two names; only one is registered. Two studies regressed against the unregistered copy, which means the right number with provenance that cannot resolve. No rule says a file under raw_indices/ must be either registered or removed, so nothing objected.

Medium

The two governing documents are kept in sync by hand, and the project memory says that fails

CLAUDE.md records that the digest “has gone stale three times already this session from exactly this drift.” The remedy currently in place is a written instruction to remember. Nothing checks it. Giving every standing rule a stable identifier and checking that both files carry the same set of identifiers would make the drift impossible to ship rather than merely discouraged.

What I would change

Seven amendments, in the order I would make them. The first is the only one that matters in the long run; the rest are instances of it.

A meta-rule, so the next gap closes itself

Adopt one general rule rather than another specific one: a standing rule that can be checked must be checked by something outside the thing it governs, and a self-attested boolean is never a check. Every failure in this repository’s history is an instance of this — the composite beta, the technical read that went stale behind a rule protecting it, the digest drift, and now the eight unchecked SIGCM clauses. The protocol has learned the lesson three times in particular and never once in general.

Turn forecast_ground_up into a record, exactly as beta was

Require a driver_record.json naming, for every revenue line: the physical unit, the disclosed source it came from, the price or rate, and the cost-per-unit basis — or an explicit flagged gap with its reason where the disclosure genuinely stops. Then assert_ground_up() inspects the record. The studies already write all of this in prose in section 1.6; this only asks them to write it once more in a form a machine can refuse. Repeat for the other seven clauses in the order they matter.

Write the rule that makes the gates compulsory

Add to both documents: every study must call assert_sigcm(), assert_beta_provenance() and assert_model_study() in its own committed code, and a repository-level job verifies that it did. The job now exists (scripts/check_study_provenance.py, in CI as a ratchet); the rule needs to say so, or the next study will not know it applies.

Specify the beta record’s schema and reject anything else

Mandate the fields assert_beta_provenance() needs — regressor file as a raw_indices/ path, index as-of date, n, R², standard error, the usability verdict, the tier landed on, and the interim note where one applies. A record missing a field fails, rather than passing on the strength of what it happens to contain.

Stamp the standard version on every study

Declare a current standard version in the protocol; require each study to record the version it was built to; have the gate report any study built to an older one. This is what lets you answer “the technique is better now” with a list instead of a feeling, and it is what makes a re-issued study finished rather than finished-for-now.

Make the index directory self-policing

One index, one filename, registered. A .csv under raw_indices/ that is not in EXCHANGE_INDEX must be either registered or deleted — with the single documented exception now in place for the Dubai series, which is held deliberately and says so.

Give every standing rule an identifier, and check both files carry it

Tag each rule ([R-BETA-01], [R-SIGCM-02] and so on) in both documents, cite the tag from the code that enforces it and from the QC gate that reports it, and add a check that the two files hold the same set of tags. It makes the hand-sync verifiable, lets a QC gate cite the rule it is testing, and gives an amendment one obvious place to land.

What not to change

The reasons. Almost every rule in the protocol names the failure it came from — FERTIGLB’s inverted valuation, COMI’s spot of 142 beside a narrative reading 129.25, nu=Gaussian parsing cleanly and dying at import. That is why the rules are obeyed when they are met at all, and it is the single most unusual thing about this repository. Every amendment above should arrive with its own reason attached, in the same voice.

The dual-framing discipline, and stop-and-inform. Both are rules about honesty rather than method, and both are working: the studies that fall short of the standard say so, in the delivered document, in plain language. The audit found the non-conforming studies mostly by reading their own confessions.
